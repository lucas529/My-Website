Context

Plug-in solar (PIPV) is a newer category in the US: small solar units you can plug straight into a wall outlet, no electrician required. That convenience is also the risk, since a normal outlet was never meant to have power pushed back into it. At CMU's CyLab I'm working with Professor Anthony Rowe on a safety device for exactly this problem. We started it from scratch, and I still am the only person working on it.

Approach

The device has two halves. The first is a contactless current sensor that sits near the breaker and reads how much current is flowing without ever touching the wire, by taking advantage of how the breaker is built inside. The second is an adapter that sits between the solar unit and the wall outlet. The two talk to each other, and if the sensor sees something unsafe, the adapter cuts the solar unit off from the house circuit.

Build

Most of my time so far has gone into the sensing side, and into a way to test it honestly. For the sensor I wound my own coil around a ferrite core to pick up the current, feeding a small signal-conditioning circuit so I can actually read it. To validate any of it safely, I'm building a realistic mock household electrical panel, real breakers and wiring, with electric space heaters as the load, so I can push known currents through it and see what the sensor reports. A lot of the early work was also just reading, since power systems were new to me, and I understand them a great deal better now.

Where it's at

The project is still in progress. The test bench is almost built and parts are still arriving, and the next steps are digitizing the sensor's signal and building the logic that decides when to shut the solar unit off. It's my first real research project, from a blank page to a physical test bench, and it's taught me how much of engineering is figuring out what to even build first.
